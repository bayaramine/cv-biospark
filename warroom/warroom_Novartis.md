| **Interview Q&A** | Q: What is ASC4KIDS and how did you contribute to it? <br> A: ASC4KIDS (NCT04925479, CABL001I12201) is a multicenter open-label Phase Ib/II study to determine the dose and safety of asciminib in pediatric CML-CP patients aged 1–18y, previously treated with ≥1 TKI. Unlike a classic DLT-based Phase I, the primary dose selection is **PK-driven**: we want to find the pediatric weight-based dose (starting at 1.3 mg/kg BID, fed) that achieves AUC comparable to adult 40 mg BID (fasted) — because adult efficacy is well established (ASCEMBL, X2101), and CML biology is identical in children and adults, allowing full efficacy extrapolation. My contribution was the statistical design and regulatory submissions. I calibrated the BLRM prior from adult X2101 data for the safety monitoring framework, ran simulation studies to characterize operating characteristics, and prepared the statistical sections of the PIP (EMA/PDCO) and iPSP (FDA/PREA) submissions. The study started enrolling December 2021, and I left Novartis in January 2023, so the analysis will be done by my successors. <br><br> Q: Why is the primary endpoint PK rather than DLT in this study? <br> A: Because the dose-limiting question is not "is this dose too toxic?" but "does this dose deliver the right exposure?" Asciminib has an established adult safety profile from years of X2101 data and the ASCEMBL Phase III. The unknown in pediatrics is the PK: body weight, metabolic rates, hepatic enzyme maturity, body composition, and oral absorption characteristics all differ between children and adults, so an adult flat dose would not necessarily deliver the right exposure in a small child. The weight-based starting dose (1.3 mg/kg BID) was determined by allometric scaling from adult popPK. Part 1 (4–6 patients) measures actual AUC at 28 days — if AUC matches the adult 40 mg BID target, the dose is confirmed and expansion begins; if not, the dose is adjusted. Safety is monitored in parallel via BLRM/EWOC, but it's not the primary driver of dose selection. <br><br> Q: What is the difference between PIP and iPSP? <br> A: A PIP (Paediatric Investigation Plan) is required by the EMA under EU Paediatric Regulation 1901/2006 for all new medicinal products before marketing authorization is sought. It must be agreed with PDCO (EMA's paediatric committee) before adult Phase III studies are completed. An iPSP (initial Pediatric Study Plan) is the FDA equivalent under PREA — required for NMEs and new indications, submitted after Phase I with FDA agreement required before starting pediatric studies. For asciminib, both were filed. The key practical difference: EMA's PIP process is more prescriptive (specific study design agreed upfront), while FDA's iPSP allows more flexibility in design evolution. Filing them jointly with maximum alignment minimizes regulatory risk — a study accepted by both PDCO and FDA avoids having to run two different pediatric studies. <br><br> Q: How does BLRM differ from BOIN, which you used at J&J for ramantamig? <br> A: Both are Bayesian dose-finding tools, but at very different levels of modeling complexity. **BLRM** is model-based: it fits a full two-parameter logistic regression of DLT probability on log(dose) via Bayesian MCMC, uses all accumulated data, incorporates an informative prior calibrated from adult data, and applies EWOC as a safety constraint. It is data-hungry (benefits from prior information), requires Bayesian modeling expertise, and is most valuable when external data (adult PK/safety) can meaningfully inform the prior. **BOIN** is model-assisted: it uses simple pre-specified decision rules based only on the observed DLT rate at the current dose — escalate if rate below lower boundary, de-escalate if above upper boundary, stay otherwise. No model fitting, no prior, no posterior. BOIN is transparent, robust, easy to implement and explain to clinicians, and explicitly endorsed in FDA's 2019 model-assisted design guidance. For ASC4KIDS, BLRM was appropriate because the safety monitoring component benefited from the rich adult prior. For ramantamig at J&J, BOIN was the right choice: a new first-in-class trispecific in first-in-human, where we had limited prior information and wanted a design that the clinical team and FDA could understand and trust intuitively. |
